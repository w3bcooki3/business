Why it takes thirty-six hours

Gunnar gets breakfast.

At 7 pm the night before the night before, we feed the levain fresh rye flour and water. By morning it has doubled and smells like green apples and yogurt.

Flour, water, patience.

We mix the flour and water alone and let it rest an hour (bakers call it autolyse) before adding levain and salt. The gluten starts organizing itself without being bullied.

Four folds, by hand.

Every 45 minutes the dough is stretched and folded over itself in the tub. It goes from shaggy to silky. No mixer arms, no shortcuts.

Shaped, then cold.

Each loaf is shaped, set in a linen-lined basket and slid into the walk-in at 4 °C for eighteen hours. The cold slows the yeast and lets the bacteria build flavor — the tang, the depth, the long finish.

Into the deck oven.

Straight from cold to a 260 °C stone deck with steam. Forty-five minutes, until the crust is properly dark. We bake it darker than you think. You’ll thank us.

Hannah Lindqvist

Grew up between Gothenburg and Ithaca, apprenticed at a bakery in Stockholm’s Södermalm, then spent two years at a stone mill in Vermont learning what happens to grain before it becomes flour.

She opened Ninebark — named for the native shrub growing by the door — in 2019, in a former laundromat on Vanderbilt. The washing machines are gone; the terrazzo floor stayed.

“If it’s not sold out by two, I made too much.”
